L5 · 05 · Leading AI Adoption in Teams

The last skill isn't technical: it's getting a team to actually use AI well, measuring whether it helped, and setting policy people follow. Most AI rollouts fail not on the tech but on adoption — no training, no measurement, unrealistic promises, or a ban that pushes people to shadow AI. This page is the playbook.

Level 550 minTracks 8, 19

By the end of this page you can

  • Run an AI adoption playbook: baseline, pilot, train, scale, measure.
  • Measure productivity honestly — without falling for the vanity metrics.
  • Design training that sticks (pairing, workflows, champions).
  • Write a sane AI usage policy people will actually follow.
  • Handle the human concerns: skill atrophy, job anxiety, uneven adoption.

Why rollouts fail

Failure modeWhat happens
Buy licences, declare victoryTools installed, nobody's workflow changes, usage decays to zero
Hype the numbers"30% faster!" with no baseline — credibility dies at the first bad output
Ban itPeople use personal devices/accounts with no controls — worse than governed use
Mandate itPeople perform compliance, resentment builds, gains don't
Ignore the humans juniors stop learning, seniors fear displacement, quiet resistance

The adoption playbook

  1. Baseline first. Before any AI, measure how long key tasks take and their quality. Without it, "AI saved us time" is unprovable.
  2. Pilot small. One willing team, real work, a few weeks. Find the workflows where AI genuinely helps.
  3. Train on workflows, not features. "Here's how to review a PR with AI" beats "here's what the chat box does".
  4. Scale with champions. Early adopters who share real wins (and failures) teach better than any deck.
  5. Measure against the baseline. Honestly — see below.

Measuring productivity honestly

The hard truth about "X% faster" Vendor studies claim huge gains; independent studies of experienced devs on real codebases show far more modest — sometimes even negative — net effects once review and rework are counted. Your own baseline is the only number that matters.
MetricTrapBetter
Lines of codeAI generates lots of code — much of it wrong or unnecessaryTasks completed, cycle time
Keystrokes/acceptance rateAccepting suggestions ≠ correct codeDefect rate, rework rate
Surveys ("I feel faster")Feels faster even when it isn'tObjective task time + quality
Speed aloneFaster code, worse quality = net lossSpeed AND quality AND cost together

Measure time, quality and cost on the same tasks, before and after. That's a believable number.

Training that sticks

  • Pair people on real tasks with an AI tool — learning by doing beats watching slides.
  • Teach workflows, not features: "review this PR", "debug this test", "write this migration" — the actual task shapes.
  • Share prompts and patterns as team assets — a shared prompt/skill library compounds (L2·01, L2·08).
  • Internal docs and golden examples — this very site is the kind of artefact that works.
  • Make room to fail cheaply — a safe sandbox where experimenting is expected, not risky.

Writing the policy

  • What's allowed: approved tools/models, what data may be used (the gateway's approved list, L4·06).
  • What's not: secrets, unreleased IP, personal data, anything regulated — named explicitly.
  • Disclosure: when must AI-generated output be labelled or reviewed?
  • Review duty: the human is accountable for AI output they ship. No "the AI did it".
  • Keep it short and positive: "here's how to use it well" gets followed; "never do anything" gets ignored.

The human concerns

  • Skill atrophy: if AI does all the writing, juniors never build the skill to review it. Deliberately keep "no-AI" practice and rotate who does the hard parts by hand.
  • Job anxiety: be honest about what AI changes; hiding it breeds distrust. The durable skills are judgement, review and system design — exactly what this curriculum teaches.
  • Uneven adoption: a few power users pull ahead; pair them with others rather than letting a gap ossify.
Try it (60 min)
  1. Take one real team task. Measure it without AI (time + quality), then with AI. Write the honest delta.
  2. Draft a 1-page AI usage policy for a team you know. Would people actually follow it?
  3. Sketch a 4-week adoption plan for that team: pilot group, training format, success metrics.

Key takeaways

  • Adoption fails on people and measurement, not the tech.
  • Baseline before AI, pilot small, train on workflows, scale with champions, measure honestly.
  • "X% faster" claims are unreliable; measure time + quality + cost on real tasks yourself.
  • Short, positive policy; the human who ships AI output stays accountable for it.
